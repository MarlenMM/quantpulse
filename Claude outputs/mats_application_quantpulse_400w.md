I couldn't tell whether I'd learned anything real about markets, or was riding beta. I started trading in March 2026, mostly AI names, and by June was up about 40% — until I noticed the S&P 500 and Nasdaq had just had a historic run, and my holdings had risen with them. That gap between "I made money" and "I understand why" made me start building QuantPulse: a sandbox to answer one question before trusting my judgment — signal, or the tide?

QuantPulse is an equity-research and portfolio-management system (marlenmm.github.io/quantpulse), built over seven weeks — July 21–September 6, 2026, 182 commits, 150–200 hours. Eight free data sources feed a SQLite database (23 tables, 13 migrations). A from-scratch layer scores every S&P 500 company across seven categories — technicals, fundamentals, analyst consensus, sentiment, momentum, macro, and smart-money — into one rank across six investor profiles, plus four forecasting methods, a bootstrap-based backtester, and three portfolio optimisers. Output: 1,554 tests, CI/CD, and two front ends (Streamlit, React+FastAPI). I used Claude Code as pair-programmer, owning architecture, methodology, and output review.

Directing an AI coding agent shifted my work from writing code to catching non-crashing errors — look-ahead bias, a category silently scored zero instead of dropped from its weighting — which otherwise corrupt results. Free sources cover price history for 98.8% of S&P 500 companies but only 49% of delisted ones, and one such stock's adjusted close jumped from $0.005 to $305.00 in one bar from a bad adjustment. A backtest is only as honest as its data.

My oversight was weakest where it mattered. The nightly data refresh kept working, but the live-demo publish check failed silently for 13 straight nights from August 10 — a locator matched six elements instead of one (a ticker collision), failing the deploy while data updated unnoticed. I only caught it when the demo's freshness indicator read "24 days stale" on September 6. I'd designed the app to be honest about data quality but hadn't held my release process to that standard.

The weekly data section — fundamentals, analyst consensus, sentiment — has never completed a full run; the first attempt hit GitHub Actions' six-hour job limit scoring articles through FinBERT. Until then, the score runs mostly on technicals and momentum, and the backtest can only grade that part. The delisted-price gap has no fix: some survivorship bias is baked into any backtest on free data.
